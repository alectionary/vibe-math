\begin{abstract}
Can the errors in a multi-trait quantitative-trait-locus model stay correlated,
after environment and block effects are removed, and still be recovered from
the covariance? Mi, Eskridge, Wang, and coauthors (2010) fit a structural
equation model that lets some traits influence others. In their simulation
the model detects three loci more often than least squares: powers of 64\%,
77\%, and 96\%, against 57\%, 69\%, and 94\%. We proved which error models
on their printed three-trait pattern are recoverable, and we reran the
simulation. Uncorrelated errors determine the coefficients. One extra error
correlation does not. Dropping one direct path and freeing that correlation
does, and the estimator is consistent. The printed equation~(7)
reproduces Table~2, including the total effect $0.75$. It does not
reproduce Table~1. Our detection rates are $50.8\%$, $53.5\%$, and $86.1\%$.
\end{abstract}

\section{What this replication did}
\label{sec:did}

This section answers what we contributed, as distinct from what Mi et al.\
published. We contributed finished proofs, a set of simulations, and the
comparison in Table~\ref{tab:compare}. Their contribution is the model, the
simulation design, and Tables~1 and~2. Commands are in
Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}.

\subsection{The Lean formalization}

The proofs say which error models on the printed pattern can be recovered
from the covariance, and which estimators converge. They are finished.
Nothing is left as a placeholder.

Lean is the proof assistant. The sources are in
\sha{formalization/correlated-residuals/CorrelatedResiduals/}.
The toolchain is Lean~4.22.0 and Mathlib v4.22.0, built with
\texttt{lake build}. The only axioms are \texttt{propext},
\texttt{Classical.choice}, and \texttt{Quot.sound}. The first is
propositional extensionality. The second is the axiom of choice, used for
the matrix inverse and for positive-definiteness. The third is the
construction of the real numbers. \ev{our own Lean proof}

The statements, in plain words, are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{recover\_sigma} (\texttt{Diagonal.lean}:246). If the trait
errors are uncorrelated, the coefficients are a function of the covariance.
$\Psi$ is the error covariance. $\Phi$ is the covariance of the locus
genotypes. $\Sigma$ is the covariance of all observed variables.
\item \texttt{bow12\_same\_law} (\texttt{Collision.lean}:114), and the same
argument for the other two pairs. Adding exactly one error correlation makes
the model non-unique: several parameter values produce the same covariance.
\item \texttt{recover\_bow} (\texttt{BowFree.lean}:187),
\texttt{recover\_bow01} (\texttt{BowMissing.lean}:148), and
\texttt{recover\_bow23} (\texttt{BowMissing.lean}:353). A bow-free pattern
drops one direct path and frees the error correlation of that pair, following
Brito and Pearl (2002). Each of the three such patterns determines the
coefficients.
\item \texttt{unitUpper\_factor\_unique} (\texttt{Triangular.lean}:68) and
\texttt{sigmaRec\_injective} (\texttt{Triangular.lean}:298). The same
uniqueness holds in every dimension, not only for three traits.
\item \texttt{plug\_in\_consistent\_ae} (\texttt{Law.lean}:174) and
\texttt{plug\_in\_bow\_consistent\_ae}
(\texttt{BowConsistency.lean}:270). The estimator that plugs the sample
covariance into the recovery formula converges almost surely. This is not a
central limit theorem.
\end{itemize}
The Gaussian standard-error formula is a derivative plus a numerical check
in \texttt{delta\_method.py}. It is not one of the Lean proofs. The proof
text is in Appendix~\ref{app:lean}, taken from commit
\sha{05d69ea141adf3d5ee936d3b48061bde241250dc}.

\subsection{The simulation studies}

The simulations ask whether the published power advantage appears under the
design the 2010 paper states. They are Monte Carlo experiments, not proofs.

Three studies are in \texttt{simulation/studies.py}. Study~1 is a
100-replicate scan that places one minus sign in front of the whole
coefficient matrix. That placement was a misreading, kept so the later
correction can be compared with it. Study~2 fits the bow-free model of
Section~\ref{sec:did}: 400 replicates, seed \texttt{20101028}. Forcing the
errors to be uncorrelated estimates the missing path as $0.8\rho$, where
$\rho$ is the correlation that was actually present. The bow-free estimator
covers the true coefficients at about the nominal 95\% rate (about $0.915$
to $0.958$). Study~3 only checks scale. It refits the paths to three
correlations from Campbell et al.\ (2003) and is not a replication of
Table~1. \ev{our own simulation}

Further runs change one choice at a time (\texttt{simulation/gaps.py} and
\texttt{simulation/assumptions.py}) or move the first locus and the sample
size (\texttt{qtl1\_search.py}). Each run records whether the choice is one
the simulation paragraph actually states.

Table~1 reports SEM powers 64, 77, and 96. We found 50.8, 53.5, and 86.1.

Table~1 reports LS powers 57, 69, and 94. We found the same rates as for the
SEM, because the two likelihood ratios are the same statistic on this model.
The verdict is no match. Section~\ref{sec:semls} explains the discrepancy.

Table~1 reports single-trait powers 6, 3, and 97 on the first trait. We
found 3.2, 1.2, and 88.6. The verdict is a partial match: the first two
cells are close, and the third is not. Placing the minus sign in front of
the whole matrix had given 13\% for that third cell. Measuring the sign
positions raises it to 86.2\% (seed \texttt{20101041}) and does not reach 97.

Table~2 reports a total effect 0.75 of the third locus on the first trait.
We found $3/4$. The verdict is a match. The matrix is
\[
(I-M)^{-1}G
=\begin{pmatrix}1/8&0&3/4\\ 3/8&-7/16&3/8\\ 1/4&-1/8&1/4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Section~\ref{sec:resolved} records the character positions that fix the signs.

Table~2 reports $-0.435$ and $0.065$ for two effects of the second locus.
We found $-7/16=-0.4375$ and $1/16=0.0625$. The verdict is close. The
difference is small enough to be a rounding or transcription discrepancy.
Ordinary three-decimal rounding of our values does not produce the printed
decimals, so that reading is not settled. Section~\ref{sec:rounding} keeps
the graded account. The same two printed decimals appear in the 2010
\emph{Genetics Research} companion.

No run that keeps the printed positions, the printed sample size of 150, and
the printed threshold reproduces 64, 77, and 96 together. The nearest triple
is 65.2, 73.3, and 99.3, from a first locus at 24~cM and 250 lines. That
design is not the design in the simulation sentence.

\section{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

This section answers which choices are ours rather than the paper's. The
answer is the five subsections below. A choice the simulation paragraph
states is not listed. Section~\ref{sec:gaps} turns the choices off one at a
time.

\subsection{Design}

We had to supply the breeding scheme and the map function, because the 2010
simulation paragraph does not. The population is an F1 selfed for twelve
generations, with residual
heterozygotes coded $\pm 1$ at equal probability. Mi et al.\ (2010) say
recombinant inbred lines and do not give the generation count or a
segregation-distortion model. The map function in the SAGMB scan is Haldane.
The 2008 conference note names Haldane and prints the infinite-RIL expansion
$2r/(1+2r)$; the SAGMB simulation paragraph does not. Cofactors are every
marker except the flanking pair, which is the sentence on PDF page~10.
The conditional expectation of the QTL genotype given the flanking markers
follows Haley and Knott (1992), as the methods section says, implemented as
the exact two-point expectation on the twelve-generation pedigree.
\ev{assumption, flagged}

\subsection{Map positions}

Marker coordinates and the detection window are ours. The locus positions
are the paper's. Markers are placed at $0,10,\ldots,100$~cM. The sentence says eleven evenly
spaced markers on a $100$~cM chromosome. Putting a marker at each end is a
reading of ``evenly spaced,'' not a coordinate the paper prints. QTLs are
placed at $22$, $42$, and $78$~cM, which the sentence does print. The scan
step is $1$~cM. A detection is counted in a window of $\pm 10$~cM around the
true position, and the position standard deviations use that window. The
paper's position standard deviations are consistent with a window nearer
$\pm 5$~cM, which the text does not state. The $\pm 10$~cM window is ours.
\ev{assumption, flagged}

\subsection{Sample size}

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:compare} uses more replicates than Table~1,
and the same number of lines. The SAGMB simulation uses $150$ lines and $100$ replicates. The baseline
powers quoted above use $1000$ replicates of that $150$-line design, so that
Monte Carlo error is smaller than the gap under discussion. The $100$-replicate
size is what Table~1 itself is. Companion designs that were simulated and
then set aside are $250$ lines (Mi et al., Genetics Research, 2010) and
$300$ lines (the 2008 conference note). Neither is the SAGMB sample size.
\ev{assumption, flagged where the replicate count exceeds the printed $100$}

\subsection{Thresholds}

The baseline uses the two critical values the paper prints. A multi-trait detection uses $\chi^2_{0.005,4}=14.86$. A single-trait
detection uses $\chi^2_{0.005,2}=10.60$. Both numbers are printed. Treating
$14.86$ as a LOD score, or as a $\chi^2$ critical value on $3$ or on $1$
degree of freedom, was tested and is not an assumption of the baseline.
The permutation threshold, a $95\%$ quantile near $16.1$ under the baseline
population, was computed for comparison and is not the threshold in the
scan that produced $50.8$, $53.5$, and $86.1$.
\ev{the printed thresholds, used as printed}

\subsection{Readings of equation~(7)}

Two coefficient matrices were programmed. Only the printed one matches
Table~2. The other is an extraction error, not a second printing of the
display. Figure~\ref{fig:eq7} is the display.

The earlier program put one minus in front of $M$ and took every entry of
$G$ as positive, so $B=-M$. Here $B$ is the matrix of paths among traits,
$M$ is the printed coefficient matrix, and $G$ holds the locus effects.
The total effect of QTL3 on $Y_1$ is then $1/4$, and the single-trait power
for that cell is $13\%$ at $100$ replicates. QTL means quantitative trait
locus. $Y_1$ is the first trait. This run is study~1.

The printed equation is $y=My+Gx+\varepsilon$, with
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2&1/4\\0&0&-1/2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
G=\begin{pmatrix}-1/8&1/4&1/2\\1/2&-1/2&1/2\\1/4&-1/8&1/4\end{pmatrix},
\]
so $B=M$, and $\Psi=\mathrm{diag}(1.6,1.8,2.5)$ as printed in equation~(8).
The baseline, the joint search, and the Table~2 arithmetic use these
printed signs. The next section shows the page and says why the other
matrix is not a reading of it.
\ev{taken from the printed page}

\section{Ambiguities that were resolved}
\label{sec:resolved}

This section answers whether the minus signs in equation~(7) are ambiguous
in print. They are not. Each minus is an ordinary unary sign against one
entry. The text layer of the same page is a different matter: it separates
those signs from the entries. The printed signs reproduce Table~2's $0.75$
and remove most of the single-trait gap on the third locus. They do not
produce the SEM powers in Table~1.

\subsection{The sign in front of \(M\)}

The page is not ambiguous. Figure~\ref{fig:eq7} is equation~(7) from the
ARS manuscript, cropped from a 600-dpi rendering of page~10. The equals sign
is followed immediately by the parenthesis of $M$. There is no minus between
them. Figure~\ref{fig:eq7minuses} enlarges the four minus signs. Each one
ends on the first digit of a single entry: $G_{11}=-1/8$, $M_{23}=-1/2$,
$G_{22}=-1/2$, and $G_{32}=-1/8$. None of them sits in the gap between
columns, and none of them spans that gap.

Three matrices were worth computing. Only the third is printed.

The original misreading puts the minus in front of $M$ and takes every
magnitude as positive:
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2&1/4\\0&0&1/2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
G=\begin{pmatrix}1/8&1/4&1/2\\1/2&1/2&1/2\\1/4&1/8&1/4\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=-M.
\]
The total effect of the third locus on the first trait is $1/4$, not the
$0.75$ in Table~2. At $100$ replicates the single-trait power for that cell
is $13\%$, where Table~1 prints $97\%$, and the SEM powers are about $59$,
$61$, and $86$.

Dropping every minus, and not inventing a leading one, uses the same
positive magnitudes with $B=M$. The total effect is $7/8$, not $0.75$.
At $100$ replicates the SEM powers are $68$, $57$, and $86$, and the
single-trait power for the third locus on the first trait is $87$. This is
another extraction of the text layer. It is not a second way to attach the
ink in Figure~\ref{fig:eq7minuses}.

The printed signs are
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2&1/4\\0&0&-1/2\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
G=\begin{pmatrix}-1/8&1/4&1/2\\1/2&-1/2&1/2\\1/4&-1/8&1/4\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=M.
\]
Then $(I-B)^{-1}G$ has $(1,3)$ entry $3/4$, which is the $0.75$ in Table~2.
The same arithmetic gives $-0.4375$ and $0.0625$ where Table~2 prints
$-0.435$ and $0.065$ (Section~\ref{sec:rounding}). Under these signs, $150$
lines, and the printed threshold, the SEM powers are $50.8$, $53.5$, and
$86.1$, not $64$, $77$, and $96$. The single-trait power for the third
locus on the first trait is $86.2\%$ at $1000$ replicates, against the
printed $97$.

\subsection{SEM power above least-squares power}
\label{sec:semls}

Table~1 reports higher SEM power than LS power on a model where the two
tests are the same statistic. On the recursive model the multi-trait SEM likelihood ratio and the
multi-trait least-squares likelihood ratio are the same statistic. In the
baseline profiles the maximum absolute difference of the mean profiles is
about $7\times 10^{-14}$. Table~1 nevertheless prints SEM powers $7$, $8$,
and $2$ points above the least-squares powers ($64$ against $57$, $77$
against $69$, $96$ against $94$). Figure~2 does not print a numeric scale
that would let those gaps be read off the plot.

Equal likelihood ratios make equal powers under a common threshold. A
difference of that size, repeated at all three QTLs, is a source discrepancy
under our reading of the two columns. It would also arise if the two columns
were computed under different thresholds or different cofactor sets. The
simulation paragraph describes one scan. The present runs do not separate
those possibilities. \ev{source discrepancy (our reading)}

\section{What we proved}

This section answers the identification question in theorem form. On the
printed pattern, uncorrelated errors are recoverable, one extra error
correlation is not, and each bow-free pattern is recoverable and consistently
estimable. The proofs are finished: Lean's \texttt{sorry} placeholder does
not appear. The axioms are \texttt{propext} (propositional extensionality),
\texttt{Classical.choice} (the axiom of choice), and \texttt{Quot.sound}
(the construction of the real numbers). \ev{our own Lean proof}
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{recover\_sigma}: uncorrelated errors and a positive-definite
genotype covariance determine every coefficient.
\item \texttt{bow12\_same\_law} and its two siblings: one extra error
correlation is not unique, for every matrix of locus effects.
\item \texttt{recover\_bow}, \texttt{recover\_bow01}, \texttt{recover\_bow23}:
each of the three bow-free patterns determines the coefficients.
\item \texttt{unitUpper\_factor\_unique} and \texttt{sigmaRec\_injective}:
the same uniqueness holds in every dimension.
\item \texttt{plug\_in\_consistent\_ae} and
\texttt{plug\_in\_bow\_consistent\_ae}, with the two further bow-free
patterns: independent samples with finite coordinate products make the
plug-in estimator almost surely consistent.
\end{itemize}
Standard errors use a delta-method derivative and Isserlis' formula for the
covariance of a sample covariance, checked in \texttt{delta\_method.py}.
\ev{our own written proof, and our own simulation}
That argument is not a Lean central limit theorem.

flanking markers. The printed critical values are 14.86 and 10.60.
\ev{taken from a cited source}
Those two numbers are the chi-square $0.005$ quantiles on 4 and 2 degrees of
freedom ($14.860$ and $10.597$). The paragraph on PDF page~9 writes the
Bonferroni approximation $\chi^2_{\alpha/M,\,m+1}$. Ten marker intervals and
$\alpha=0.05$ give the tail probability $0.005$, with $m+1=4$ for three
traits and $m+1=2$ for one trait. \ev{our own calculation, from the cited formula}

The paper does not state the following, so each is our assumption.
\ev{our assumption}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The map function is Haldane, with no interference.
\item An RIL is an F1 selfed for 12 generations. A leftover heterozygote is
coded $\pm 1$ with equal probability. There is no segregation distortion.
\item The eleven markers are at $0,10,\ldots,100$~cM.
\item The analysis uses the centered covariance with divisor $n$, which is
the fitting function~(6). We did not run SAS PROC TCALIS.
\item The flanking expectation is the conditional mean of the QTL genotype
under that same 12-generation law. The 10~cM window mentioned later in the
paper is for the wheat scan, not for this simulation, and was not used.
\item A named QTL is detected when the maximum likelihood ratio in
$[0,32]$, $(32,60]$, or $(60,100]$ exceeds the threshold. Effects are read
at that argmax.
\end{enumerate}
Seeds are \texttt{20101027} for this study. The outputs are
\texttt{study1.json}, \texttt{study1\_power.csv}, and
\texttt{study1\_effects.csv} under \texttt{simulation/out}.

For a complete recursive order, the Gaussian likelihood of the diagonal
structural model equals the likelihood of the multivariate regression on the
same exogenous regressors: the sum of the successive residual log variances
is the log determinant of the residual covariance. The two joint likelihood
ratios are therefore the same number. In the run, the maximum absolute
difference of the two mean profiles is $7\times 10^{-14}$.
\ev{our own written proof, confirmed by our own simulation}
Mi et al.\ describe the two profiles as very close (PDF page~14). Their
Table~1 powers still differ by 7, 8, and 2 replicates out of 100.

$250$ lines or by controlling the other QTL genotypes, and those two
adjustments have not been found together in one design the text states.

\section{Correlated residuals}

This section answers what goes wrong if the errors are correlated and the
fit still forces them to be independent. The uncorrelated fit invents a
direct path. The bow-free fit, which drops that path and frees the error
correlation, recovers the coefficients. The bow drops the edge into trait~0 from trait~2, sets that structural
coefficient to zero, and frees the residual covariance $c=\rho\sqrt{1.6\cdot 2.5}$.
The other equation-(7) coefficients stay. The exogenous variables are the
three QTL genotypes on the RIL map, not a marker scan.
\ev{our assumption, matching \texttt{recoverBow}}
The sample is the centered second-moment matrix, $n=150$, $400$ replicates,
seed \texttt{20101028}.

At the population covariance the bow plug-in recovers the parameter to
machine precision. The diagonal plug-in does not. Its probability limit for
the missing edge is $0.8\rho$: zero at $\rho=0$, $0.20$ at $\rho=0.25$, and
$0.48$ at $\rho=0.6$. The QTL coefficient $G_{11}=1/8$ has probability limit
$0.075$ at $\rho=0.25$ and $0.005$ at $\rho=0.6$. The coefficient $G_{13}=1/2$
moves to $0.45$ and $0.38$. \ev{our own simulation}
A Wald test that the missing edge is zero, using the diagonal delta-method
standard error, rejects at rate $0.058$ when $\rho=0$ and at rate $0.728$,
$0.877$, and $1$ at $\rho=0.25$, $0.30$, and $0.60$. The joint nine-degree
Wald test of all QTL coefficients is only mildly inflated at $\rho=0.25$
(rejection rate $0.065$) and reaches $0.297$ at $\rho=0.6$. The damage shows
up first as bias in two coefficients and as a spurious edge, not as an
immediate explosion of the joint QTL test.

\texttt{recoverBow} is centered on the QTL and path coefficients. At
$\rho=0.25$ the Monte Carlo means of $b_{12}$, $b_{23}$, $G_{11}$, and
$G_{13}$ are $-0.507$, $-0.498$, $0.127$, and $0.509$. Coverage of the
nominal 95\% delta-method intervals, with the Gaussian Isserlis covariance
plugged into the sample, is between $0.915$ and $0.958$ across the reported
coefficients and the six correlations. The genotypes are $\pm 1$, so
Isserlis is an approximation. \ev{our assumption inside the standard error}
The coverage near 95\% says the approximation is tolerable at $n=150$ for
these coefficients. Variance-component means sit a little low ($1.54$ against
$1.6$ at $\rho=0$) because the likelihood uses divisor $n$; the probability
limit is still the population value.

The same seeds were rerun with the position-reading coefficients: $b_{12}=+1/2$,
$b_{23}=-1/2$, the edge $b_{13}$ removed from $+1/4$, and the signed $G$.
\ev{our own simulation}
At $\rho=0.25$ the diagonal probability limit of the missing edge is again
$0.20$ and the Wald rejection rate is $0.728$. \texttt{recoverBow} means are
$b_{12}=0.493$, $b_{23}=-0.498$, $G_{11}=-0.123$, and $G_{22}=-0.508$, with
coverage between $0.920$ and $0.955$. The sign reading changes the parameter
values and does not change the bias or the coverage.

\section{Calibration to three phenotypic correlations}

This section asks whether three published wheat correlations can stand in
for the missing line data. They cannot. The run is a scale check, not a
replication of Table~1. Campbell et al., Crop Science~43 (2003), Table~2, print phenotypic
correlations for 95 recombinant inbred chromosome lines pooled across seven
environments. There are no standard deviations and no environment or block
adjustment. \ev{taken from a cited source, with the limits just stated}
The text layer of the copy we could read dropped minus signs. The paragraph
above the table says that SPSM and kernels per square meter were negatively
correlated with thousand-kernel weight. We therefore use
$\mathrm{Corr}(\mathrm{TKWT},\mathrm{SPSM})=-0.44$. The cells
$\mathrm{Corr}(\mathrm{TKWT},\mathrm{KPS})=0.08$ and
$\mathrm{Corr}(\mathrm{KPS},\mathrm{SPSM})=0.59$ are the signs in that text
layer; the prose does not restate them. \ev{our assumption for those two signs}
GYLD is the fourth trait in the application and is not in equation~(7). The
three-trait order is $Y_1=\mathrm{TKWT}$, $Y_2=\mathrm{KPS}$,
$Y_3=\mathrm{SPSM}$, which is the chain used in the indirect-effect
arithmetic on PDF page~17 of Mi et al. \ev{our assumption}

Treating the correlation matrix as a covariance, the diagonal recursive
factorization is
\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.521&-0.747\\0&0&0.590\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\Psi=\mathrm{diag}(0.629,0.652,1).
\]
\ev{our own calculation}
The eigenvalues of the correlation are $0.224$, $1.077$, and $1.699$, so
this sign choice is positive definite. Naive Gaussian standard errors that
pretend the matrix is a sample correlation of 95 i.i.d.\ lines are about
$0.10$ for the two coefficients in the first equation and $0.084$ for
$0.590$. Campbell pooled seven environments, so those standard errors are not
his sampling variances. The point fit does not depend on $N$.

The scan was rerun with this $B$ and $\Psi$ and with the signed $G$ from the
position reading of equation~(7). Seed \texttt{20101029}, 100 replicates. Those
QTL coefficients were not estimated from the correlations, and on this
residual scale they are large: multi-trait power is 93\%, 95\%, and 99\%.
Single-trait power for QTL3 on $Y_1$ is 96\%. That is a consequence of the
scale mixture, not evidence that the 2003 correlations produce Mi's powers.
\ev{our own simulation, under an assumption we flag}
The bow check at $\rho=0.25$ (seed \texttt{20101030}) gives a diagonal
probability limit $0.198$ for the missing edge and a rejection rate $0.74$.
The unsigned-$G$ run of the same seed had multi-trait powers 100\%, 96\%, and
100\%. The sign change does not turn the calibration into a replication.

\section{Limitations}

This section states what the proofs and the simulations do not settle. The
wheat data fit is blocked, the sampling distribution is not a Lean theorem,
and the SEM powers in Table~1 remain unmatched. The wheat fit is still blocked. It needs the 98 line identifiers, the ANOVA
residuals of grain yield, thousand-kernel weight, kernels per spike, and
spikes per square meter, the $\pm 1$ QTL and cofactor codes, the divisor-$n$
sample covariance, and the edge set as a matrix. Figure~5 draws arrows and
does not print that matrix. Campbell's Table~2 does not substitute for any
of those columns.

The Drton--Foygel--Sullivant criterion is not a theorem of this repository.
The delta method is not a Lean central limit theorem. The position reading of
equation~(7) meets Table~2's parameter block up to the two rounded QTL2
entries. It leaves the SEM powers for QTL2 and QTL3, and about eleven points
of the single-trait QTL3 power, short of Table~1. A second block tested
the choices the simulation paragraph does not pin down. None of them
closes the QTL2 gap. A $250$-line scan matches that one cell and misses
the first QTL. Those leftovers are not Monte Carlo error at $1000$ replicates. Twelve
generations of selfing, Haldane's map, the marker placement, and the
detection windows are our assumptions. The 2021 Hidalgo-Contreras wheat SEM
is a different experiment.
